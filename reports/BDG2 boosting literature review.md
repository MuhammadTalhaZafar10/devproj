# BDG2 boosting papers measure interpolation, not forecasting

Only one post-2021 peer-reviewed paper benchmarks the full tree-ensemble family — Random Forest, AdaBoost, GBM, XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost — on the Building Data Genome Project 2 under a single pipeline: Xie and Tummala (2025), *Sensors* 25(23):7294, which uses 10 buildings and 175,440 hourly observations, all from one site judging by the shared `Panther_` meter prefix ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). That paper also supplies the number that should anchor any new BDG2 proposal: **random k-fold cross-validation inflates R² to 0.90–0.99, while forward-chaining time-series validation yields 0.24–0.34, a ΔR² of 0.63–0.74 for all six models** ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Everything else in the post-2021 BDG2 literature uses boosting as a tool inside a forecasting, transfer-learning, anomaly-detection or classification pipeline, and validation splits are frequently random, unspecified, or pooled across buildings ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14107); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00525)). The documented pitfalls are therefore not speculative: random-CV inflation is quantified, rolling-window leakage is documented by name but never priced in building energy, leave-one-site-out generalisation has no published BDG2 estimate at all, and simulated sensor drift inflates RMSE by **327–586%** in the one paper that tests it ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). The consequence is that a new study claiming a better booster is entering a contest the literature has already declared a tie: CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM and GBM are pairwise statistically indistinguishable on BDG2 (all p > 0.05) ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). The defensible claim is narrower, cheaper and unoccupied — **price each leakage channel separately on a multi-site BDG2 panel** — and it is buildable in two days.

Three patterns matter for positioning. First, **classification in this literature means building-attribute classification, not consumption-level classification**: a title search across all 337 citing works for "classif*" returns zero hits, and Raymand et al.'s "performance class" pipeline is the only explicit class label ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2023.113327)). Second, **boosting is under-used relative to Transformers** in the 2023–2026 window, which makes a GBDT-based study cheap to run but not automatically novel. Third, the cohort changes with cleaning: 3,053 meters becomes 1,828 in Fu et al., 1,636 buildings in Raymand et al., 327 buildings in Yan et al., so **cross-paper metric comparison is not like-for-like** ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00525); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2023.108127)). Note also that BDG2 is **3,053 meters from 1,636 non-residential buildings across 19 sites**, 2016–2017 hourly, ~53.6 M measurements ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-020-00712-x)) — not "3,053 buildings," a confusion the base paper itself propagates when it says "1578 buildings" ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)).

## Chronological splits cut R² to 0.24–0.76, and the base paper reports both

The base paper's clean-weather ranking is conventional and unsurprising: CatBoost wins on every error metric, LightGBM and XGBoost follow within noise, Random Forest trails, and AdaBoost is worst on RMSE while oddly reporting a higher R² than Random Forest. The magnitudes are the useful part, because they set the honest comparator band for any new BDG2 study.

| Model | RMSE (kWh) | MAE (kWh) | R² | MAPE (%) | Train time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random Forest | 6.62 | 4.23 | 0.591 | 9.6 | 57.73 |
| AdaBoost | 9.00 | 6.65 | 0.661 | 13.3 | 216.87 |
| GBM | 6.33 | 4.04 | 0.709 | 8.9 | 416.49 |
| XGBoost | 6.12 | 3.95 | 0.692 | 8.8 | 2.28 |
| LightGBM | 6.06 | 3.95 | 0.723 | 8.6 | 3.35 |
| CatBoost | **5.67** | **3.82** | **0.764** | **8.3** | 3.41 |

Source: Xie & Tummala 2025, Table 14, per-building simple average over 10 buildings, 70/30 chronological split ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Per-building RMSE ranges from **3.2 kWh/h** (small retail) to **12.8 kWh/h** (large education), and CatBoost is lowest in **8 of 10** buildings. Friedman test χ²(5) = 19.3, **p = 0.002**; Nemenyi post-hoc gives CatBoost vs AdaBoost p < 0.001 and CatBoost vs RF **p = 0.027**, while every pairwise comparison among CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM and GBM returns **p > 0.05** ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Read plainly: the only statistically defensible model claim on this subset is "CatBoost beats the bagging and classical-boosting baselines," not "CatBoost beats modern GBDTs." All six are standard implementations rather than bespoke architectures — bagging over randomised trees ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1010933404324)), classical boosting ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1006/jcss.1997.1504)), gradient boosting ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1013203451)), and three modern frameworks ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1145/2939672.2939785); [Source](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/6907-lightgbm-a-highly-efficient-gradient-boosting-decision-tree); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09516)) — so the table measures defaults and tuning effort, not architectural novelty.

The protocol contrast is where the paper earns its citation weight. Under random k-fold the same six models sit at R² 0.90–0.99; under 5-fold forward chaining they sit at 0.24–0.34, with per-model gaps of 0.628 (GBM) to 0.738 (XGBoost) ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Modern GBDTs show *larger* gaps than classical boosting, which the authors attribute to more aggressive fitting of temporal autocorrelation. The mechanism is stated explicitly: shuffled folds place hour-adjacent samples and their lag features on both sides of the split, so the model recognises rather than forecasts ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)).

Two caveats belong in any slide that quotes these numbers, and they are the kind of detail a lab audience will ask about. First, **Table 14 and Table 17 are not the same estimator**. A 70/30 chronological holdout with per-building averaging gives R² 0.59–0.76; 5-fold forward chaining gives 0.24–0.34. Reporting "chronological BDG2 R² ≈ 0.7" and "chronological BDG2 R² ≈ 0.3" are both defensible and they describe different experiments; a new paper must say which. Second, **the forward-chaining standard deviations (±0.69 to ±0.83) are larger than the means and sit outside the [0,1] range normally implied by R²**, which is only possible if per-building R² values go strongly negative — the paper's own RF result includes "occasional negative R² for volatile sites" ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Either the SD is mislabeled or the mean is not a safe point estimate for a single building; both readings argue for reporting fold-level and building-level distributions instead of a headline mean.

The R² non-monotonicity is a third trap worth naming, because it generalises beyond this paper. AdaBoost reports R² = 0.661 with RMSE = 9.00 kWh, while Random Forest reports R² = 0.591 with RMSE = 6.62 kWh. That is arithmetically impossible for a pooled R² on a common target and entirely possible when you average per-building R² across buildings whose variances differ and some of which score negative. **Mean-of-per-building R² is not R² of pooled predictions**, and averaging it equally across a retail kiosk and a large education building weights a 3 kWh meter the same as a 13 kWh meter. This is the cheapest, most credible methodological critique available to a new study, and it costs nothing to fix.

For external calibration, the GEPIII error analysis is the strongest anchor: across the top-50 solutions, **79.1%** of test rows fall within RMSLE_scaled ≤ 0.1, **16.1%** between 0.1 and 0.3, and **4.8% exceed 0.3**, a tail the authors call "unlikely to be accurately predicted" from historical meter, weather and basic metadata ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/23744731.2022.2067466)). Comparable non-BDG2 evidence points the same way: a single university building at 15-min resolution with a chronological split gives LightGBM R² = 0.938 one-step and 0.884 four-step, with Random Forest at 0.868 and training time 1,944 s versus LightGBM's 11.2 s ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/en17061450)), while a fused LightGBM + XGBoost + CatBoost model reports RMSLE 0.1898 and R² 0.9745 across 13 residential locations — a residential nowcast, not a BDG2 result ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-91767-6)). Any BDG2 result above R² ≈ 0.9 under a temporal split on heterogeneous buildings should be treated as a leakage signal, not a modelling triumph.

## Four pitfalls, and only random-CV inflation is quantified

**Random CV inflation** is the one channel with a number attached, and the number is large: ΔR² 0.63–0.74 on BDG2, with the same paper reporting 0.12–0.26 for glucose and 0.42–1.21 for PM2.5 ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). It is not an isolated finding. A cross-field survey documents leakage in **17 fields and 294 papers**, defines an eight-type taxonomy, and shows that correcting leakage erased the apparent advantage of complex ML over logistic regression in one re-analysis ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804)). A four-dataset connectome study finds leakage magnitude is dominated by **feature selection and repeated subjects** rather than by split choice, and that small datasets amplify it ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46150-w)) — a direct warning for BDG2 studies that select features on the full panel before splitting. The important nuance is that cross-validation is not inherently invalid; the original methodological argument concerns *how* CV is applied to autoregressive predictors ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ins.2011.12.028)), and the standard remains rolling-origin evaluation with a gap ([Source](https://otexts.com/fpp3/tscv.html)). Leakage also propagates through pipeline stages rather than living in the split alone ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-025-11326-3)), and a 2025 position paper extends the concern to benchmark integrity itself, where dataset reuse and temporal overlap between correlated series produce optimistic estimates ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13654)).

**Rolling-window leakage** is named everywhere and priced nowhere in building energy. Three hazards get collapsed into one: centred windows that use *t+1…t+k* (a hard leak); windows ending at *t* used to predict *t+1* (legitimate, but it makes the model a persistence forecaster and inflates apparent skill relative to operational feature availability); and scalers or imputers fitted on the full series (a silent, ubiquitous leak). The base paper uses "lagged consumption (1–24 h), rolling statistics (6/12/24-h mean/std/min/max)" but **does not state whether the rolling window ends at *t***, so its own numbers cannot be classified as clean or leaky from the text; its weather normalisation, by contrast, is correctly fitted on each building's training period only ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). General treatments exist — causal-alignment principles ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1145/2382577.2382579)), evidence that non-causal decomposition preprocessing produces "abnormally superior but misleading results" ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06986)), leakage-free rolling decomposition in wind power ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.energy.2024.133513)), and a 119-study review naming "insufficient reporting of preprocessing procedures and temporal leakage prevention" as a major gap ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/en19122937)). A 2025 preprint quantifies the sequence-construction channel at **up to 20.5% RMSE inflation under 10-fold CV** but typically under 5% for temporal splits ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06932)) — indicative only, not peer reviewed. No building-energy paper isolates the rolling-statistic contribution.

**Cross-building generalisation** is studied as a transfer-performance question, never as an evaluation-validity question. The transfer literature establishes that source–target pairing and data volume drive gains more than architecture does ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2020.114499); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2022.112461)), that domain-adversarial training beats target-only baselines ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.energy.2020.119208)), that cross-building prediction with few physical parameters is its own benchmark problem ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2021.117829)), and that generalisation to unseen houses is achievable with enough history ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/pr8040484)). On BDG2 itself, pre-training on 327 buildings improves fine-tuned accuracy but produces documented **negative transfer** ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2023.108127)), and transformer transfer gains on 16 BDG2 datasets range from **0.6% to 25.5%**, with the large figure reserved for targets under 10 buildings ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2025.115632)). What is missing is the protocol contrast: **no paper reports the same model under within-building, leave-one-building-out, and leave-one-site-out splits with a common metric**. This gap is sharper than it first appears because the base paper's 10 buildings all carry the `Panther_` meter prefix, which indicates a **single site**; its 0.24–0.34 temporal R² therefore shares building identity *and* site, weather and utility conventions between train and test, making it an optimistic estimate of unseen-building skill (inference from the meter IDs reported in [Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). BuildingsBench shows the community already treats zero-shot-on-unseen-buildings and fine-tuning as distinct benchmark tasks, which legitimises the framing ([Source](https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-0871)).

**Drift robustness** has one fully specified protocol and a striking result. Three perturbation families at four intensities — Gaussian noise at σ = 0.05–0.20 × SD, impulse corruption of 10–40% of values, and cumulative "calibration drift" at 0.5–2.0 kWh/h — produce RMSE inflation of **2.6–27.0%** (Gaussian), **29.3–157.4%** (impulse) and **327–586%** (drift) ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). The rank reversal is the finding to carry forward: AdaBoost is worst on clean data (9.00 kWh) yet **most drift-robust (327%)**, while CatBoost, the clean winner, degrades **488%** ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Degradation is nearly constant across intensity levels, implying structural rather than magnitude-dependent failure. One terminology trap deserves explicit flagging: the base paper's "calibration drift" means sensor bias accumulation, not probability-calibration drift, and conflating the two is common. Continual learning for building energy is an active but separate thread ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2023.121481)), as is online ensemble learning evaluated explicitly under concept drift ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1109/access.2021.3095420)), and reviews already concede that "we are now running the risk of overfitting to these particular datasets at the cost of broader generalization" while calling for backtesting or time-series CV to prevent spurious results ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2023.110407)).

The unifying observation is that all four pitfalls are properties of the **protocol**, not the model, and the literature's own reviews ask for reporting discipline rather than new architectures ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/en19122937); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2025.114612)). That is why a two-day study can contribute: the expensive part of the field is not fitting boosters, it is agreeing on what a number means.

## Five defensible framings, one buildable in two days

| # | Framing | Core experiment | Novelty against the literature | 1–2 day feasibility | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Price each leakage channel separately** | {random k-fold, blocked-time, leave-one-building-out, leave-one-site-out} × {no target-derived features, lags only, rolling stats ending at *t*, centred/leaky} × 2–3 boosters; report ΔR² per channel with per-building distributions | Base paper reports **one composite ΔR²** on 10 single-site buildings and cannot separate temporal autocorrelation, building identity and feature construction ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)); no paper found does the decomposition | **High** if scoped to ~100–200 stratified buildings (≈2–3.5 M rows) | Full 53 M-row panel is not 2-day work; must scope and say so |
| 2 | **Consumption-level classification under site holdout** | Define Low/Medium/High from training-year EUI or load-factor quantiles; classify held-out buildings and held-out sites; report macro-F1, balanced accuracy and per-class recall | **Zero** "classif*" titles among the 337 citing works; nearest precedents are building *performance class* ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2023.113327)) and quantile benchmarking lines ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/su15065211)) | **Medium** — label definition, imbalance handling and a leakage guard do not fit two days defensibly | Class boundaries are arbitrary quantiles; with lag features the bins are nearly deterministic, so accuracy inflates |
| 3 | **Robustness as a rank-stability matrix** | Replicate noise/impulse/drift at 4 intensities on more buildings; report model-rank instability instead of a winner | Base paper's rank reversal is single-paper, 10 buildings ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)); an independent replication is publishable either way | **High** — protocol is fully specified and reusable | Reads as replication unless paired with a new axis (e.g., site stratification) |
| 4 | **Separate sensor drift from calibration drift** | Covariate drift stress tests plus temporal reliability/coverage of predictive intervals (quantile LightGBM or conformal) | The two are conflated across the literature; no BDG2 treatment found | **Medium** — needs probabilistic wrapper and coverage metrics | Scope creep; conformal + drift + decomposition is a three-week paper |
| 5 | **"CatBoost/LightGBM is best on BDG2"** | Six-model table on a new subset | Already contradicted: modern GBDTs pairwise p > 0.05, and GEPIII top solutions clustered tightly ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)) | Trivial | **Not novel**; reviewers will cite the base paper against you |

**Recommendation: framing 1, scoped as a protocol-contrast decomposition rather than a full-panel census.** The claim it supports is precise and citable: *the BDG2 literature reports a single composite inflation number for random versus temporal validation; we decompose that gap into a temporal-autocorrelation component, a building-identity component, a site/weather component, and a feature-construction component, and show which dominates.* That claim has no direct precedent. The base paper's ΔR² conflates the channels by construction; the connectome evidence predicts feature construction may rival split choice ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46150-w)); BuildingsBench establishes that seen/unseen is already a legitimate task distinction ([Source](https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-0871)).

The build is realistic. Day one is cohort and feature construction: sample buildings stratified by site and primary use, cap at roughly 150–200 meters to keep the matrix near 3 M rows, build four feature tiers (target-free calendar/weather/metadata; plus lags; plus rolling statistics ending at *t*; plus a deliberately leaky centred variant), and fit scalers inside folds. Day two is fitting and reporting: three models (LightGBM, Random Forest, and CatBoost as the strongest clean performer) across four protocols and four feature tiers, which is 48 fits at roughly seconds-to-minutes each, then per-building ΔR² distributions and a rank-stability panel. Report RMSE in kWh, MAE, MAPE, R² computed on pooled predictions *and* per-building, plus the fold-level spread. The single most valuable figure is a 4 × 4 heatmap of ΔR² with per-building violin plots beneath it; the single most valuable sentence is which channel accounts for the largest share.

Two design choices protect the claim. First, **hold the model fixed while varying the protocol**, because the literature already shows model choice is a statistical tie on this dataset. Second, **include a deliberate leak as a positive control** — if the centred-rolling variant does not inflate R² sharply relative to the causal variant, the pipeline is broken, and that check is what separates a decomposition from an assertion. The four-tier feature table doubles as the reporting checklist: it states, per feature, the timestamp at which the value becomes available, which is the building-specific instantiation of the eight-type leakage taxonomy ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804)). If time permits, frame 3's drift protocol is a natural add-on because its parameters are fully specified and it uses the same feature matrix; the combined "protocol sensitivity + drift rank reversal" story is stronger than either alone and still fits a long week, not a long month.

Framing 2 deserves its own paragraph, because it is the strongest *content* gap and the weaker *two-day* build. No paper in the citing set puts "classif*" in its title, and the closest work classifies building attributes rather than consumption levels ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2023.113327)); the nearest methodological precedents are quantile-based benchmarking lines ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/su15065211)) and pattern-classification-then-forecast pipelines ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2021.110929)). A Low/Medium/High classifier is therefore genuinely unoccupied, but it is also the task most vulnerable to the pitfalls above: hourly bins with a lag-1 feature are almost deterministic, class frequencies follow building type, and the bin edges are analyst-chosen quantiles that a reviewer can move. Attempting it in two days produces a high-accuracy table that proves nothing. The right sequencing is decomposition first, then classification *under* the leakage-free protocol as the follow-on paper — at which point the contribution is not the bins but the demonstration that published-style classification accuracy on BDG2 collapses under site holdout.

## What you can say tomorrow without overclaiming

Three sentences survive scrutiny. First: *"Only one post-2021 peer-reviewed study benchmarks the six tree-ensemble families on BDG2, on 10 buildings from a single site, and it reports a random-versus-temporal ΔR² of 0.63–0.74 while finding modern GBDTs statistically indistinguishable"* ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25237294)). Second: *"No published BDG2 study reports leave-one-site-out generalisation, and no building-energy study prices the rolling-window leakage channel, so the reported skill of the field is an upper bound of unknown tightness"* — this is an absence-of-evidence claim from API searches of OpenAlex, Crossref and arXiv, not a systematic review, and should be stated that way. Third: *"Because the protocol, not the booster, moves R² by more than half a point, the useful contribution is a decomposition of that movement, not another architecture."*

The verification caveats need to travel with the slides. The general web-search endpoint was unavailable during evidence collection (**HTTP 402**), so discovery ran through OpenAlex, Crossref, Europe PMC and arXiv APIs; Elsevier journals do not deposit abstracts in Crossref or OpenAlex, so **six of the fourteen table entries are marked † and their dataset attribution is unconfirmed** — do not put Yan et al., Stjelja et al., Piscitelli et al., Canaydin et al., Fan et al. or Yang et al. on a slide as "BDG2 studies" without a full-text check. The Dai & Huang 2025 paper on LightGBM/RF/XGBoost with optimisation strategies (DOI 10.1007/s00202-025-03167-8) is topically ideal but was unreachable behind a paywall, so its dataset and metrics are omitted rather than guessed. OpenAlex indexing lags by weeks, so 2026 preprints may be missing. And the base paper has three internal inconsistencies that a careful presenter should name before a reviewer does: "1578 buildings" versus the descriptor's 1,636; a headline "33.1% improvement over AdaBoost" that does not reconcile with its own table (9.00 → 5.67 kWh is a 37.0% reduction); and Table 14 versus Table 17 both labelled chronological while differing by a factor of two in R².

Expect three objections and answer them directly. *"Ten buildings is too small"* — correct, which is why the proposed design uses 100–200 meters stratified across sites and reports per-building distributions rather than a mean. *"Your ΔR² is just the base paper's ΔR²"* — no: the base paper reports one composite number and cannot attribute it, and its 0.24–0.34 is still optimistic for unseen buildings because building identity is shared. *"Why boosting and not a Transformer?"* — because the comparison is protocol-invariant, boosting trains in seconds, and GEPIII's top solutions were GBM/LightGBM ensembles with preprocessing rated important by over 70% of respondents ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02898)). The honest weakness to concede in the introduction is that no review explicitly lists "decompose the random-versus-temporal CV gap" as future work, so the novelty argument has to be constructed from the base paper's single composite number and the reporting gaps the reviews do name ([Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/en19122937)) — that is a weakness of the *framing*, not of the *experiment*.

## Conclusion

The BDG2 boosting literature is not short of models; it is short of denominators. Six boosters on 10 single-site buildings already produce a statistical tie, a 0.63–0.74 random-versus-temporal gap, a rank reversal under drift, and an R² column that cannot be reconciled with its own error columns — all in one paper. Every subsequent study that reports a better booster on a random or unspecified split is adding a number whose meaning is unknown, which is why gains of 0.6–8.2% from architecture or transfer changes sit inside the noise created by cleaning and split choices.

The strategic implication for a lab with two days is that the cheapest defensible contribution is the one the field has made structurally impossible to cite: attribute the inflation. A study that holds the model fixed, varies protocol and feature availability factorially, and reports per-building ΔR² distributions converts a known warning into a measured quantity, and it does so on data that costs nothing to download. If that experiment lands, the follow-on paper — leave-one-site-out generalisation with drift stress tests — writes itself, and it will have a baseline the current literature cannot supply.
